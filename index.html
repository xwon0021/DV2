A data visualisation by Wong Xin Thung · 9 October 2026

Behind Australian-made products are local businesses, specialised industries and a changing workforce. Where are they concentrated, and how does their contribution reach beyond the factory?

Manufacturing data provide context for Australian-made products; they do not identify every locally made product or establish Australian Made certification.

01

Place and concentration

Where manufacturing happens

Australian-made products begin with local businesses. But a large manufacturing cluster and a high concentration are different things: one counts businesses, while the other compares them with the resident population.

These maps move from local areas to individual reporting facilities. ABS Statistical Areas Level 2 (SA2s) show business rates and counts; NPI locations then show where reporting facilities operate.

02

Sector structure

What Australia makes

Location is only the beginning. Food contributes A$31.279bn, or 22.03% of the combined manufacturing subdivision value added in 2024–25. Its role in employment is even larger.

Compare workforce share with economic contribution, then explore each state’s industry mix. Sales growth and economic profiles show why a large industry is not necessarily a fast-growing one.

V4

Does workforce share translate into industry value?

Australian manufacturing subdivisions, 2024–25

Blue circles show employment share; orange diamonds show industry value added (IVA) share. Each connector compares two 2024–25 shares, not change over time. Positive Δ means IVA share is larger; negative Δ means employment share is larger.

Food provides 25.7% of manufacturing employment but 22.0% of IVA. Machinery and equipment shows the reverse: 11.8% of employment and 14.2% of IVA. Workforce size and economic contribution do not follow the same pattern; IVA is not sales or profit.

Food leads nationally, but its importance varies by state. Tasmania’s workforce shows a particularly strong specialisation.

V6

Sales growth and value-added growth

Year-on-year percentage change across manufacturing subdivisions, 2023–24 to 2024–25.

Key Finding · Income vs Value Added Divergence

Primary metals sales grew 23.8%, while IVA fell 3.1%. The 26.9 percentage point gap shows that higher sales did not translate into higher value added. The chart shows the difference, but does not establish its cause.

Sales growth is nominal and includes price changes. IVA reflects net economic contribution rather than gross revenue or profit.

Primary metals show that sales and value added can diverge. Compare all four measures next to see each industry’s broader profile.

03

Change over time

How manufacturing has changed

Manufacturing employment recovered from 830.2 thousand in 2019–20 to 900.8 thousand in 2024–25. Yet it remained 10.4% below its 2006–07 level of 1,005.5 thousand.

The national total hides different industry paths. Start with the employment trend, then follow the rankings: food remained the largest manufacturing employer throughout the period shown.

04

Beyond the factory

Australian-made in context

Manufacturing reaches beyond finished consumer goods. In 2023–24, construction received A$74.215bn in domestic intermediate inputs from manufacturing, its largest outward relationship shown here.

Follow these domestic links, compare recent changes in industry value added, then look overseas. Finish with consumers: how does the economic picture connect with stated interest in buying Australian-made products?

Industry value added: 2023–24 versus 2024–25

Key Takeaway · Industry Value Added Trajectory

Food recorded the largest absolute IVA gain: A$2.348bn, reaching A$31.279bn. Textiles, leather, clothing and footwear fell A$336m to A$2.216bn. A sector’s overall performance can hide very different industry outcomes.

Industry changes affect the domestic picture. Export connections add another perspective: which overseas markets receive manufactured merchandise?

V13

What Australians say about buying local

Roy Morgan’s March 2025 findings distinguish wanting, intending and looking to buy local, alongside the importance of knowing a product’s origin.

84%

More likely to buy when they know a product is Australian Made.

Conditional stated likelihood — not measured purchases.

20 people = 100% · each person represents 5 percentage points of respondents. Partial fill shows the exact remainder. Symbols represent percentage points, not individual people.

These survey findings overlap and cannot be added together. They describe stated attitudes, not observed purchases. The source does not report sampling details or confidence intervals.

Key Takeaway · Consumer Sentiment

Knowing a product’s origin matters to respondents. While 84% report greater likelihood of buying Australian Made, 67% intend to buy more in the next year. These are separate, overlapping findings, not steps in a purchasing funnel.

Australian-made products connect local clusters, specialised industries and changing jobs with domestic and overseas demand. Consumers report strong interest in local origin; actual purchasing behaviour remains a separate question.
