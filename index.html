Australian
Made Products

What sits behind an Australian-made product? Local businesses, people at work and connections across Australia and overseas. Their story begins with where manufacturing happens, then follows the industries and changes that shape it.

Manufacturing data provide context for Australian-made products. They do not identify every locally made product or establish Australian Made certification.

01

Place and concentration

Where manufacturing happens

Australian-made products begin in real places, from busy industrial areas to smaller regional centres. A place can have many manufacturing businesses, or a high number compared with its population. Those are two different stories.

The first map shows where manufacturing stands out relative to the number of residents. Business counts then reveal the largest clusters, while reporting facilities give a closer view of individual locations.

02

Sector structure

What Australia makes

The places on the maps do not all make the same things. Food manufacturing contributed A$31.279bn in industry value added (IVA) in 2024–25. IVA measures value produced minus goods and services used. Food’s share of manufacturing jobs exceeds its share of IVA.

Workforce size and economic contribution do not always move together. Each state has its own industry mix, and a large industry can have slower growth than a smaller one.

Does a larger workforce mean a larger share of economic value?

Australian manufacturing industry groups (called subdivisions), 2024–25

Blue circles show employment share. Orange diamonds show industry value added (IVA) share. Each connector compares two 2024–25 shares, not change over time. Positive Δ means IVA share is larger. A negative Δ means employment share is larger.

Food provides 25.7% of manufacturing employment but 22.0% of IVA. Machinery and equipment shows the reverse: 11.8% of employment and 14.2% of IVA. Workforce size and economic contribution do not follow the same pattern. IVA is not sales or profit.

The national picture hides local differences. Each state has its own manufacturing mix, and some depend more heavily on particular industries.

Sales growth and value-added growth

Year-on-year percentage change across manufacturing subdivisions, 2023–24 to 2024–25.

What the data shows · Income vs Value Added Divergence

Primary metals sales grew 23.8%, while IVA fell 3.1%. The 26.9 percentage point gap shows that higher sales did not translate into higher value added. The chart shows the difference, but does not establish its cause.

Sales growth is nominal and includes price changes. IVA reflects net economic contribution rather than gross revenue or profit.

Sales and value added capture different parts of industry performance. Bringing jobs and wages into the comparison gives a fuller picture.

03

Change over time

How manufacturing has changed

Today’s industry mix is only a snapshot. Manufacturing employment fell to 830,200 in 2019–20 before recovering to 900,800 in 2024–25. Even after that recovery, it was still 10.4% below the 2006–07 level.

Industries followed different paths within that national trend. Food remained the largest employer throughout the period shown, although a steady rank can hide changes in job numbers. Recent changes in value added show another side of industry performance.

V10

Industry value added: 2023–24 versus 2024–25

Food recorded the largest dollar gain in value added from 2023–24 to 2024–25, while some industries declined. These dollar changes add context to the growth percentages shown earlier.

What the data shows · Industry Value Added Trajectory

Food recorded the largest absolute IVA gain: A$2.348bn, reaching A$31.279bn. Textiles, leather, clothing and footwear fell A$336m to A$2.216bn. A sector’s overall performance can hide very different industry outcomes.

Manufacturing’s role extends beyond its own workforce and economic value. Other Australian industries use the materials and goods it produces.

04

Beyond the factory

Australian-made in context

Jobs and industry totals capture only part of manufacturing’s role. Manufacturers also supply materials and goods that other industries rely on. In 2023–24, construction used A$74.215bn in domestic manufacturing inputs, the largest outward connection shown here.

Those connections extend overseas through exports. Back at home, consumers also express interest in buying Australian-made products. These are different views of manufacturing’s place in the economy, from industry supply chains to what shoppers say matters to them.

V13

What Australians say about buying local?

Roy Morgan’s March 2025 findings distinguish wanting, intending and looking to buy local, alongside the importance of knowing a product’s origin.

84%

More likely to buy when they know a product is Australian Made.

Conditional stated likelihood — not measured purchases.

20 people = 100% · each person represents 5 percentage points of respondents. Partial fill shows the exact remainder. Symbols represent percentage points, not individual people.

These survey findings overlap and cannot be added together. They describe stated attitudes, not observed purchases. The source does not report sampling details or confidence intervals.

Main message · Consumer Sentiment

Knowing a product’s origin matters to respondents. While 84% report greater likelihood of buying Australian Made, 67% intend to buy more in the next year. These are separate, overlapping findings, not steps in a purchasing funnel.

Behind Australian-made products are local business clusters, industries with different strengths and a workforce that has changed over time. Their reach extends into other Australian industries and overseas markets. Consumers’ interest in local origin adds a human perspective, although what people say they would buy is not the same as what they actually purchase.
